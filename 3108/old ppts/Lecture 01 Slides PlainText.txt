SLIDE 2: THE CORE PHILOSOPHY OF THIS COURSE
Why Are We Here?
- The Limitation of Medicine: Hospitals cannot cure poverty. Surgeons cannot operate on a lack of education. Pills cannot fix a toxic neighborhood.
- The Shift: Moving from reacting to biological sickness to investing in societal vitality.
- The Big Question: How do we design a society where people never get sick in the first place?
Exam Focus: Be prepared to contrast the Medical Model (downstream, individual, biological treatment) with the Public Health Model (upstream, environmental, systemic intervention).

SLIDE 3: THE PARADIGM SHIFT IN HUMAN SURVIVAL
The Tale of the Broad Street Pump (1854)
- The Crisis: Cholera outbreak in London.
- The Medical Response: Treating the sick, bleeding patients, blaming bad air (miasma).
- The Public Health Response (John Snow): Mapped the deaths, identified the contaminated water pump, and removed the handle.
- The Lesson: He did not invent a medicine; he changed the environment. 
Takeaway: Health promotion is the science of removing the pump handle.

SLIDE 4: DEFINING HEALTH (THE 1948 WHO DEFINITION)
A Revolutionary Concept
World Health Organization (1948):
Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.
Why this mattered:
- Broke the monopoly of the biological and medical model.
- Introduced mental and social dimensions as equal to physical health.
Critical Flaw: The word "complete". If health requires complete well-being, then anyone with a mild headache, grief, or stress is technically unhealthy. It turns health into an unattainable utopia.

SLIDE 5: SALUTOGENESIS: A BETTER DEFINITION
Aaron Antonovsky’s Paradigm (1970s)
Instead of asking "What causes disease?" (Pathogenesis), Antonovsky asked: "What creates health?" (Salutogenesis).
- Health is a Continuum, not a fixed state. We slide up and down the health continuum daily based on our resources.
The Sense of Coherence (SOC):
1. Comprehensibility: The world makes sense.
2. Manageability: I have the resources to cope.
3. Meaningfulness: Life's challenges are worth the effort.
Exam Focus: Use Salutogenesis to explain how a person with a chronic disability can still possess high health if their SOC and coping resources are strong.

SLIDE 6: HEALTH AS A RESOURCE FOR LIVING
The Ottawa Charter Re-framing (1986)
Quote: Health is a resource for everyday life, not the objective of living.
- The Car Analogy: Gas is not the goal of driving; it is the resource that gets you to your destination.
- The Human Analogy: Health is not the goal of life. The goals are to love, learn, work, and play. Health is the fuel that allows us to do those things.
- Health Capital: Like a bank account, it can be invested in or depleted by social conditions.

SLIDE 7: DECONSTRUCTING THE MEDICAL EXPERIENCE
Disease vs. Illness vs. Sickness
These are not synonyms. They represent three different realities:
1. Disease (The Doctor’s View): The biological pathology (e.g., a tumor, a virus, high blood pressure). Objective.
2. Illness (The Patient’s View): The subjective, lived experience of feeling unwell (e.g., pain, fatigue, fear). 
3. Sickness (Society’s View): The social role granted by society (e.g., getting a doctor's note, disability benefits).
Paradox: You can have a disease without an illness (asymptomatic hypertension), and an illness without a disease (chronic fatigue with normal labs).

SLIDE 8: THE ICEBERG MODEL OF DISEASE
Visual Concept: An iceberg in the ocean
- Above the Water (The Tip): Recognized, diagnosed Disease. (What hospitals treat).
- Just Below the Surface: Undiagnosed conditions, sub-clinical symptoms, and risk factors.
- Deep Below: The heavy burden of Illness that never gets a medical label.
- The Water Itself: The social environment that dictates who gets labeled with a Sickness and who is ignored.
Takeaway: Public health mostly treats the tip. Health promotion addresses the whole iceberg.

SLIDE 9: DEFINING DISABILITY
Medical Model vs. Social Model
The Medical Model:
- Disability is a broken body that needs to be fixed to fit into normal society.
- Focus: Curing the impairment.
The Social Model (The HP Standard):
- Impairment is the biological fact (e.g., inability to walk).
- Disability is socially constructed (e.g., the building only has stairs).
- Focus: Removing societal barriers and utilizing Universal Design.
Exam Focus: If a case study features a wheelchair user unable to enter a clinic, the stairs are the disability, not the legs. 

SLIDE 10: FOOD FOR THOUGHT
The "Normal" Body
- Myopia (nearsightedness) is a severe biological impairment. Yet, because society invented cheap eyeglasses, it is rarely considered a disability.
- Question: How many disabilities exist today simply because society refuses to design environments for neurodivergent brains (e.g., ADHD, Autism)? 
- Reflection: We do not need to fix the brain; we need to fix the fluorescent lights, the 8-hour desk requirements, and the rigid social norms.

SLIDE 11: HEALTH PROMOTION VS. DISEASE PREVENTION
Two Sides of the Same Coin?
Disease Prevention:
- Focus: Avoiding the bad (Pathogenic)
- Target: Specific risks and at-risk groups
- Examples: Vaccines, cancer screenings, statins
- Goal: Stop a specific pathology
Health Promotion:
- Focus: Building the good (Salutogenic)
- Target: Whole populations and broad determinants
- Examples: Paid maternity leave, safe parks, living wages
- Goal: Increase vitality and control
Takeaway: The best public health programs seamlessly integrate both.

SLIDE 12: THE PARABLE OF THE RIVER
Upstream vs. Downstream Thinking
Visual Concept: A river with people drowning
- Downstream: Diving in to rescue the drowning. (Emergency rooms, trauma care). Heroic, but exhausting and endless.
- Midstream: Pulling them out before the rapids. (Community clinics, behavior change).
- Upstream: Walking to the source to find out who is pushing them in and building a fence. (Policy, environment, systemic change).
Takeaway: Health Promotion is the science of walking upstream.

SLIDE 13: LEVEL 0: PRIMORDIAL PREVENTION
Preventing the Risk Factor from Emerging
- Target: Entire populations, societal norms, macro-environments.
- Goal: Stop the risk factor from ever existing in the culture.
- Examples: Banning trans-fats in the national food supply; designing cities so walking is the default; universal basic income to prevent the chronic stress of poverty.
Takeaway: This is the deepest, most powerful level of intervention.

SLIDE 14: LEVEL 1: PRIMARY PREVENTION
Preventing the Disease from Occurring
- Target: Healthy individuals or those with existing risk factors.
- Goal: Stop the biological pathology from starting.
- Examples: Immunizations (Measles, HPV); condom distribution (HIV prevention); mandatory seatbelt laws and hard-hat regulations; fluoride in the water supply.
Takeaway: The Shield of Public Health.

SLIDE 15: LEVEL 2: SECONDARY PREVENTION
Early Detection and Prompt Intervention
- Target: Asymptomatic individuals with early, sub-clinical disease.
- Goal: Halt progression before irreversible damage.
- Examples: Mammograms, Pap smears, colonoscopies; routine blood pressure and cholesterol checks.
Critical Concept: Lead-Time Bias. Finding a slow-growing, non-fatal tumor early does not always save a life; it may just prolong the time the person spends as an anxious patient.

SLIDE 16: LEVEL 3: TERTIARY PREVENTION
Reducing the Impact of Established Disease
- Target: Individuals with symptomatic, diagnosed, chronic disease.
- Goal: Prevent complications, restore function, improve Quality of Life (QoL).
- Examples: Diabetic foot-care clinics (preventing amputation); cardiac rehabilitation post-heart attack; stroke recovery physiotherapy.
Takeaway: We are no longer preventing the disease; we are preventing the disability and premature death associated with it.

SLIDE 17: THE PREVENTION CONTINUUM (SUMMARY)
Visual Concept: A timeline arrow
1. Primordial: No risk factors exist. (Policy/Environment)
2. Primary: Risk factors exist, but no disease. (Behavior/Shields)
3. Secondary: Disease exists, but no symptoms. (Screening)
4. Tertiary: Symptoms and disease exist. (Rehab/Management)
Exam Focus: You will be given 10 public health interventions on the midterm. You must categorize them into these four levels and justify your choice.

SLIDE 18: THE SOCIAL DETERMINANTS OF HEALTH (SDOH)
Your Zip Code is More Powerful Than Your Genetic Code
The conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age.
- Income and Social Protection
- Education
- Housing and Neighborhood Design
- Food Security
- Early Childhood Development
Takeaway: Medicine accounts for only 10 to 20 percent of health outcomes. SDOH account for the rest.

SLIDE 19: FUNDAMENTAL CAUSE THEORY
Link and Phelan (1995)
Why do health- The Sick Role: Talcott Parsons and the social contract of being ill.
- gaps persist even when we cure diseases?
- The Theory: Socioeconomic Status (SES) is a Fundamental Cause because it embodies flexible resources (Money, Knowledge, Power, Prestige, Social Networks).
- The Mechanism: When a new health threat emerges (TB in 1920, HIV in 1990, Obesity today), the wealthy use their flexible resources to protect themselves. 
- The Result: The mechanism of disease changes, but the inequity remains.
Exam Focus: You cannot educate your way out of a fundamental lack of resources. Knowledge campaigns fail if the poor lack the money and time to act on them.

SLIDE 20: THE HEALTH GRADIENT AND THE WHITEHALL STUDIES
It is Not Just Poverty vs. Wealth
- The Finding: Health operates on a continuous step-by-step gradient. The middle class is sicker than the upper class; the working class is sicker than the middle class.
- The Whitehall Studies (British Civil Servants): Everyone had a job. Everyone had the exact same universal healthcare. Yet, clerical workers had 3 times the heart disease mortality of top administrators.
- The Culprit: Allostatic Load (biological wear-and-tear) caused by low autonomy, high stress, and lack of control over one's work environment.

SLIDE 21: THE SETTINGS APPROACH
Where Health is Created
Hospitals are actually poor places for health promotion (stressful, pathogenic, expensive). Health must be integrated into everyday settings:
- Schools: Banning sugary drinks, designing active playgrounds.
- Workplaces: Flexible hours, ergonomic setups, psychological safety.
- Cities (WHO Healthy Cities): Active transport, green spaces, mixed-use zoning.
- Prisons: Addressing trauma, addiction, and social reintegration.
Takeaway: The setting itself becomes the intervention.

SLIDE 22: THE OTTAWA CHARTER (1986)
The Constitution of Health Promotion
Core Definition: The process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health.
Three Basic Strategies:
1. Advocate (for political and economic conditions)
2. Enable (reduce inequities, ensure equal opportunities)
3. Mediate (coordinate across different sectors, not just health)

SLIDE 23: OTTAWA ACTION AREAS 1 AND 2
Upstream Structural Change
1. Build Healthy Public Policy (Health in All Policies - HiAP)
- Health must be on the agenda of all lawmakers (Agriculture, Transport, Finance).
- Examples: Sugar taxes, minimum wage laws, urban zoning.
2. Create Supportive Environments
- Altering the Choice Architecture so the healthy choice is the default.
- Examples: Removing soda from checkout aisles; making stairwells bright and musical.

SLIDE 24: OTTAWA ACTION AREAS 3 AND 4
Human and Social Capital
3. Strengthen Community Action
- Doing things WITH communities, not TO them.
- Empowerment, ownership, and utilizing lived experience.
4. Develop Personal Skills
- Moving beyond basic facts to Health Literacy and life skills (coping, cooking, navigating systems).

SLIDE 25: OTTAWA ACTION AREA 5
Reorienting Health Services
- Moving from Curative Care to Health-Promoting Care.
- Social Prescribing: Doctors prescribing community gardening, debt counseling, or art classes to address SDOH.
- Systemic Shift: Moving from Fee-For-Service (rewarding sickness) to Value-Based Care (rewarding wellness).
Takeaway: Hospitals must practice what they preach (e.g., paying their cleaners a living wage).

SLIDE 26: EQUITY VS. EQUALITY VS. JUSTICE
The Moral Compass
Visual Concept: Three people looking over a fence
- Equality (Sameness): Giving everyone the exact same size box to stand on. (Fails those who need more help).
- Equity (Fairness): Giving people the specific resources they need to reach the same outcome. (Levels the playing field).
- Justice (Structural Change): Removing the wooden fence entirely and replacing it with a chain-link fence. (Dismantling the systemic barrier).
Exam Focus: Always design interventions for the most marginalized. If it works for them, it works for everyone.

SLIDE 27: THE COMMERCIAL DETERMINANTS OF HEALTH
The Corporate Playbook
Private sector activities that negatively impact public health.
- The Big Four: Tobacco, Ultra-processed Food, Alcohol, Fossil Fuels.
- Manufacturing Doubt: Funding junk science to delay government regulation.
- Health Washing: Using Corporate Social Responsibility (e.g., funding a youth sports league) as a smokescreen to avoid marketing regulations.
Takeaway: True health promotion requires regulating corporate power, not just educating consumers.

SLIDE 28: PUZZLING QUESTIONS
Critical Reflections for the Semester
1. If health is determined by zip code, is lifestyle education just a distraction from wealth redistribution?
2. Can a health promotion campaign ever be truly neutral, or is it always an imposition of dominant cultural values?
3. Is it ethical to use AI and digital surveillance to nudge citizens toward healthy behaviors?
4. How do we measure the success of a prevention program when the greatest victory is the complete absence of noticeable events?
Takeaway: Hold these tensions. They are the essence of our field.

SLIDE 31: KEY TERMS GLOSSARY (EXAM PREP)
Master the Vocabulary

- Salutogenesis: Origins of health.
