The Workplace as a Determinant of Health
- In previous lectures, we explored the life course and the broad social determinants of health.
- Today, we zoom in on one of the most powerful, daily environments shaping adult health: The Workplace.
- Objectives for today:
  1. Distinguish between Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) and Workplace Health Promotion (WHP).
  2. Understand the economic and human costs of poor workplace health.
  3. Master the core theories of psychosocial hazards (Karasek and Siegrist).
  4. Apply the WHO Healthy Workplace Framework to program design.
  5. Critique modern work trends (gig economy, remote work) through a health lens.
Exam Focus: You will be asked to diagnose a toxic workplace using theoretical models and propose structural, rather than individual, interventions.

SLIDE 3: THE WORKPLACE AS A PRIMARY SETTING FOR HEALTH
- Adults spend roughly one-third of their waking lives at work.
- The workplace is a microcosm of society, containing its own social hierarchies, physical environments, and cultural norms.
- Work provides essential resources for health: income, social status, identity, and social connection.
- Conversely, work can be a primary source of chronic stress, physical injury, and social marginalization.
Takeaway: You cannot promote population health without addressing the conditions of work.

SLIDE 4: OHS VS. WHP - A CRITICAL DISTINCTION
- Occupational Health and Safety (OHS):
  - Focuses on preventing work-related injuries and acute illnesses.
  - Deals with physical hazards (chemicals, machinery, fall risks).
  - Driven by legal compliance and regulatory mandates.
- Workplace Health Promotion (WHP):
  - Focuses on improving overall health, well-being, and vitality.
  - Addresses lifestyle factors, mental health, and organizational culture.
  - Driven by voluntary organizational commitment and employee well-being.
Takeaway: OHS keeps you from dying at work. WHP helps you thrive because of work. Both are required.

SLIDE 5: THE EVOLUTION OF WORK AND HEALTH
- The Industrial Era: Health threats were primarily physical (coal dust, amputations, extreme physical exhaustion).
- The Post-Industrial Era: Shift toward service and knowledge work. Physical hazards decreased, but sedentary behavior and cognitive load increased.
- The Digital Era: Constant connectivity, algorithmic management, and the blurring of work-life boundaries.
Takeaway: The nature of occupational hazards has shifted from acute physical trauma to chronic psychosocial and metabolic stress.

SLIDE 6: THE BUSINESS CASE FOR WHP
- Why should employers invest in health promotion?
- The moral argument: Employers have a duty of care to the humans who generate their profit.
- The economic argument: Poor employee health directly impacts the bottom line through healthcare costs, turnover, and lost productivity.
- The strategic argument: Healthy workplaces attract top talent, foster innovation, and build organizational resilience.
Exam Focus: When designing a WHP proposal, you must speak the language of business (ROI, retention, productivity) alongside the language of public health.

SLIDE 7: DIRECT VS. INDIRECT COSTS OF POOR HEALTH
- Direct Costs:
  - Employer contributions to health insurance premiums.
  - Workers' compensation claims.
  - Costs of hiring and training replacement staff.
- Indirect Costs (Often 3 to 4 times higher than direct costs):
  - Absenteeism (missing work due to illness).
  - Presenteeism (being at work but functioning at reduced capacity).
  - Management time spent dealing with underperforming or distressed staff.
  - Loss of institutional knowledge when experienced staff leave.

SLIDE 8: PRESENTEEISM - THE HIDDEN EPIDEMIC
- Definition: Employees are physically present at work but mentally or physically disengaged due to illness, stress, or burnout.
- Why it happens: Fear of job loss, toxic workplace cultures that stigmatize taking sick leave, or sheer workload pressure.
- The Impact: Presenteeism accounts for the vast majority of productivity loss in knowledge-work sectors.
- The Danger: It masks the true health burden of a workforce. A company might boast "low absenteeism" while its employees are silently burning out.

SLIDE 9: PSYCHOSOCIAL HAZARDS - THE INVISIBLE TOXINS
- Psychosocial hazards are aspects of job design, organization, and management that have the potential to cause psychological or physical harm.
- Examples: Unreasonable deadlines, lack of role clarity, bullying, poor communication, and job insecurity.
- Unlike a chemical spill, psychosocial hazards are invisible, cumulative, and often normalized as "just part of the job."
Takeaway: A toxic manager is a recognized occupational health hazard, just as much as asbestos or heavy machinery.

SLIDE 10: KARASEK'S DEMAND-CONTROL MODEL
- Developed by Robert Karasek (1979) to explain workplace stress.
- Two main dimensions:
  1. Psychological Demands: Workload, time pressure, and cognitive complexity.
  2. Decision Latitude (Control): The worker's ability to make decisions and use their skills.
- The core premise: High demands are not inherently bad IF the worker has high control to manage them.

SLIDE 11: THE FOUR JOB TYPES (KARASEK)
1. High Strain (High Demand, Low Control): The most toxic. Leads to burnout, depression, and cardiovascular disease. (e.g., call center workers, assembly line workers).
2. Active (High Demand, High Control): Leads to learning, motivation, and growth. (e.g., surgeons, senior managers).
3. Low Strain (Low Demand, High Control): Comfortable but can lead to skill atrophy.
4. Passive (Low Demand, Low Control): Leads to boredom, apathy, and loss of self-efficacy.
HP Application: To reduce stress, you do not always need to lower demands; you can increase employee autonomy and control.

SLIDE 12: SIEGRIST'S EFFORT-REWARD IMBALANCE MODEL
- Developed by Johannes Siegrist (1996).
- Focuses on the social reciprocity of work.
- High Effort: High workload, time pressure, and physical exertion.
- Low Reward: Lack of financial compensation, lack of esteem/recognition, and lack of job security/career opportunities.
- The Result: When high effort is not met with adequate reward, it triggers severe emotional distress and physiological stress responses (allostatic load).
Takeaway: People can endure incredibly hard work if they feel fairly compensated and deeply respected.

SLIDE 13: THE PHYSICAL ENVIRONMENT OF WORK
- Beyond psychosocial factors, the built environment of the workplace dictates health behaviors.
- Indoor Air Quality: Poor ventilation leads to "Sick Building Syndrome," causing headaches, fatigue, and respiratory issues.
- Lighting: Lack of natural light disrupts circadian rhythms, leading to poor sleep and mood disorders.
- Noise: Open-plan offices often create chronic cognitive fatigue due to constant background noise and lack of privacy.
HP Application: Environmental design is a powerful "Enabling Factor" in the PRECEDE-PROCEED model.

SLIDE 14: ERGONOMICS AND THE SEDENTARY WORKPLACE
- The modern knowledge worker sits for 8 to 10 hours a day.
- Prolonged sitting is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and musculoskeletal disorders, even if the person exercises after work.
- Ergonomic interventions: Sit-stand desks, ergonomic chairs, and monitor heights.
- Behavioral interventions: Mandatory movement breaks, walking meetings, and centralizing printers/trash bins to force walking.

SLIDE 15: ORGANIZATIONAL CULTURE AND PSYCHOLOGICAL SAFETY
- Culture is "how things are done around here."
- Psychological Safety (Amy Edmondson): The belief that you will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes.
- In psychologically unsafe workplaces, employees hide errors, avoid asking for help, and suppress mental health struggles until they reach a crisis point.
- WHP must target leadership training to foster psychological safety as a foundational health resource.

SLIDE 16: THE MYTH OF "RESILIENCE TRAINING"
- Many corporations respond to employee burnout by offering "resilience training," mindfulness apps, or yoga classes.
- The Critical Perspective: This individualizes a systemic problem. It tells the employee, "The workload is impossible, so you need to get better at enduring it."
- This is a form of victim-blaming in the workplace.
- True WHP fixes the workload, the deadlines, and the management practices, rather than just teaching the worker to meditate through the toxicity.

SLIDE 17: SYSTEMIC BURNOUT VS. INDIVIDUAL COPING
- The World Health Organization officially classifies Burnout as an "occupational phenomenon," not a medical condition.
- It is defined by three dimensions:
  1. Feelings of energy depletion or exhaustion.
  2. Increased mental distance from one's job, or feelings of negativism/cynicism.
  3. Reduced professional efficacy.
- Burnout is a failure of the work environment, not a failure of the worker's coping mechanisms.

SLIDE 18: THE WHO HEALTHY WORKPLACE FRAMEWORK
- The WHO provides a comprehensive model for WHP that moves beyond isolated wellness programs.
- It requires a holistic approach integrating the physical environment, the psychosocial environment, personal health resources, and community involvement.
- It emphasizes that a healthy workplace is one where workers and managers collaborate to continually protect and promote the health, safety, and well-being of all workers and the sustainability of the workplace.

SLIDE 19: THE FOUR AVENUES OF THE WHO FRAMEWORK
1. The Physical Work Environment: Addressing safety, ergonomics, and physical hazards.
2. The Psychosocial Work Environment: Addressing organizational culture, management practices, and stress.
3. Personal Health Resources in the Workplace: Providing access to health screenings, healthy food, and fitness facilities.
4. Enterprise Community Involvement: How the company impacts the local community (e.g., environmental pollution, supporting local schools, providing stable local jobs).

SLIDE 20: APPLYING THE OTTAWA CHARTER TO WHP
- Build Healthy Public Policy: Corporate policies on paid sick leave, parental leave, and the "right to disconnect" after hours.
- Create Supportive Environments: Designing offices for movement, natural light, and psychological safety.
- Strengthen Community Action: Empowering employee resource groups, peer-support networks, and union representation.
- Develop Personal Skills: Training managers in mental health first aid and leadership empathy.
- Reorient Health Services: Integrating occupational health nurses and counselors directly into the workplace.

SLIDE 21: APPLYING PRECEDE-PROCEED TO THE WORKPLACE
- Phase 1 (Social): What are the employees' quality of life goals? (e.g., work-life balance, financial security).
- Phase 2 (Epidemiological): What are the health metrics? (e.g., rates of musculoskeletal pain, depression, hypertension).
- Phase 3 (Educational/Ecological):
  - Predisposing: Beliefs about taking sick leave (e.g., "If I take a day off, I'm letting the team down").
  - Enabling: Access to mental health benefits, flexible working hours.
  - Reinforcing: Managerial praise for working weekends (negative reinforcement for health).

SLIDE 22: THE CHANGING NATURE OF WORK - THE GIG ECONOMY
- The rise of gig work (Uber, DoorDash, freelance platforms) has fundamentally altered occupational health.
- Gig workers lack the traditional "Enabling Factors" of employment: no paid sick leave, no workers' compensation, no job security, and no collective bargaining power.
- This creates extreme financial precarity, leading to high allostatic load and the tendency to work while sick or injured.
- WHP for gig workers requires macro-level policy advocacy, not just corporate wellness apps.

SLIDE 23: REMOTE WORK AND THE BLURRING OF BOUNDARIES
- The shift to remote and hybrid work has eliminated the physical commute but destroyed the psychological boundary between "work" and "home."
- The "Always-On" Culture: Employees feel pressured to answer emails at 9 PM to prove they are working.
- Health Impacts: Increased sedentary behavior, social isolation, and inability to psychologically detach from work, leading to chronic sleep disruption.
- WHP Solution: Implementing strict organizational policies regarding after-hours communication and respecting the "right to disconnect."

SLIDE 24: INTERSECTIONALITY IN THE WORKPLACE
- Workplace hazards do not affect all employees equally.
- Women often face the "double burden" of paid work and unpaid domestic/care labor, leading to higher rates of time-stress and burnout.
- Marginalized racial and ethnic groups often face "identity strain" and microaggressions in the workplace, adding a layer of chronic psychosocial hazard that privileged colleagues do not experience.
- LGBTQ+ employees may face the stress of concealing their identity or navigating non-inclusive organizational cultures.

SLIDE 25: THE GENDERED NATURE OF WORKPLACE HEALTH
- Occupational health research has historically focused on male-dominated industries (manufacturing, mining).
- Female-dominated industries (nursing, teaching, social work, care work) suffer from severe emotional labor demands and compassion fatigue, which are often unrecognized as occupational hazards.
- Furthermore, workplace policies regarding pregnancy, menstruation, and menopause have historically been ignored or stigmatized in WHP programs.

SLIDE 26: CASE STUDY - THE 4-DAY WORK WEEK
- Recent global trials of the 4-day work week (100 percent pay, 80 percent time, 100 percent productivity).
- Health Outcomes: Massive reductions in employee burnout, stress, and sleep deprivation. Significant improvements in mental health and life satisfaction.
- Business Outcomes: Revenue remained stable or increased, absenteeism dropped, and staff retention soared.
- Takeaway: Reducing total work hours is a profound upstream intervention for population health, challenging the cultural assumption that more hours equal more value.

SLIDE 27: DESIGNING A WHP PROGRAM - TOP-DOWN VS. BOTTOM-UP
- Top-Down: Management decides what the employees need (e.g., buying a ping-pong table or a meditation app). Often fails due to low uptake and perceived as tone-deaf.
- Bottom-Up: Employees are surveyed and involved in designing the interventions. High uptake, high relevance.
- The Best Approach: Participatory WHP. A joint committee of management and frontline workers co-designs the program, ensuring it addresses actual structural barriers, not just superficial perks.

SLIDE 28: THE ROLE OF LABOR UNIONS AND COLLECTIVE BARGAINING
- Historically, the greatest advancements in workplace health (the weekend, the 8-hour day, child labor laws, safety regulations) were won by labor unions, not gifted by benevolent employers.
- Collective bargaining remains one of the most powerful structural interventions for workplace health promotion.
- Unionized workplaces consistently show better safety records, higher wages, and better mental health outcomes due to the balance of power between labor and capital.

SLIDE 29: EVALUATING WHP PROGRAMS
- Process Evaluation: Did employees actually use the new ergonomic chairs? Did they attend the mental health first aid training?
- Impact Evaluation: Did perceived stress levels drop? Did employees report higher psychological safety and better work-life balance?
- Outcome Evaluation: Did absenteeism rates decrease? Did workers' compensation claims drop? Did long-term cardiovascular risk factors improve?
- Warning: Do not use individual weight loss or blood pressure as the sole metric for a WHP program. Measure organizational culture and systemic stress reduction.

SLIDE 30: PUZZLING QUESTIONS FOR REFLECTION
1. If a company offers excellent on-site gyms and healthy food, but maintains a toxic, high-demand/low-control management culture, is it a "healthy workplace"?
2. As AI and automation take over cognitive tasks, will the future of work liberate humans to pursue creative, healthy endeavors, or will it create mass job insecurity and severe psychosocial distress?
3. Should employers have the right to incentivize or penalize employees based on their personal health behaviors outside of work hours (e.g., charging higher insurance premiums to smokers)?
4. How do we promote health in the informal economy (street vendors, day laborers) where there is no formal "workplace" to intervene in?

SLIDE 31: KEY TERMS GLOSSARY
- Workplace Health Promotion (WHP): Combined efforts of employers, workers, and society to improve health and well-being at work.
- Presenteeism: Being at work but functioning at reduced capacity due to illness or stress.
- Psychosocial Hazards: Aspects of job design and management that cause psychological or physical harm.
- Demand-Control Model: Karasek's theory linking high demands and low control to high job strain.
- Effort-Reward Imbalance: Siegrist's theory linking high effort and low reward to stress.
- Psychological Safety: The belief that one can speak up without fear of punishment.
- Burnout: An occupational phenomenon characterized by exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced efficacy.
