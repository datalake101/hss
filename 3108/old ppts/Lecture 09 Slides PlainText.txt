SLIDE 2: WELCOME AND LECTURE OBJECTIVES
Reframing the Later Years
- In previous lectures, we explored the life course, workplaces, and mental health.
- Today, we focus on the final, and often most misunderstood, stage of the life course: Older adulthood.
- Objectives for today:
  1. Deconstruct ageism as a lethal social determinant of health.
  2. Understand the WHO framework for Healthy Aging and Intrinsic Capacity.
  3. Analyze the built environment, social isolation, and polypharmacy.
  4. Shift the paradigm from "burden of care" to "aging in place" and intergenerational solidarity.
  5. Apply health promotion theories to the elderly population.
Exam Focus: You must be able to critique the "medicalization of aging" and design interventions that optimize functional ability rather than just treating chronic diseases.

SLIDE 3: THE DEMOGRAPHIC REALITY
The "Silver Tsunami" is a Triumph
- The global population is aging at an unprecedented rate. By 2050, the number of people aged 60 and older will double, reaching over 2 billion.
- This is not a crisis; it is a monumental public health success story. It reflects victories in sanitation, vaccination, maternal care, and chronic disease management.
- However, our social structures, built environments, and economic models were designed for a demographic pyramid that no longer exists.
- The challenge of the 21st century is not just adding years to life, but adding life to years.

SLIDE 4: DEFINING "OLD"
Chronological vs. Biological vs. Social Age
- Chronological Age: The number of years since birth. Arbitrary and often a poor indicator of actual health or capability.
- Biological Age: The physiological state of the body's cells and systems. Can be accelerated by chronic stress or decelerated by healthy behaviors.
- Social Age: The roles, expectations, and norms society assigns to a person based on their perceived stage of life.
- Takeaway: Public policy relies on chronological age (e.g., retirement at 65), but health promotion must focus on biological and social age to be effective and equitable.

SLIDE 5: AGEISM AS A SOCIAL DETERMINANT
The Most Normalized Prejudice
- Ageism is the stereotyping, prejudice, and discrimination against people on the basis of their age.
- It is the most socially normalized of any prejudice, often more accepted in media and daily conversation than racism or sexism.
- Institutional Ageism: Mandatory retirement ages, healthcare rationing by age, and urban planning that ignores mobility limitations.
- Interpersonal Ageism: Patronizing language ("elderspeak"), ignoring the older person in a room, or assuming cognitive decline.
- Internalized Ageism: When older adults accept negative stereotypes, believing they are inherently useless or burdensome.

SLIDE 6: THE BIOLOGICAL COST OF AGEISM
How Stereotypes Get Under the Skin
- Becca Levy's groundbreaking research demonstrates that ageism literally alters human biology.
- Older adults with positive self-perceptions of aging live, on average, 7.5 years longer than those with negative self-perceptions.
- Internalized ageism increases cardiovascular stress, reduces the will to live, and decreases engagement in preventive health behaviors.
- Takeaway: Ageism is not just a social slight; it is a measurable, lethal pathogen that accelerates biological weathering.

SLIDE 7: PARADIGMS OF AGING
From "Successful" to "Healthy"
- Successful Aging (Rowe & Kahn): Focuses on the absence of disease, high cognitive/physical function, and active engagement. Critiqued for being elitist and ignoring those living with chronic disabilities.
- Active Aging: Emphasizes continued participation in social, economic, and civic affairs. Sometimes critiqued for placing the burden of "staying active" entirely on the individual.
- Healthy Aging (WHO): The process of developing and maintaining the functional ability that enables well-being in older age. This is the current, most inclusive gold standard.

SLIDE 8: THE WHO FRAMEWORK ON HEALTHY AGING
Optimizing Function Over Curing Disease
- Healthy aging is not about being free of disease. Many older adults live with multiple chronic conditions but still experience high well-being.
- The focus shifts from "curing pathology" to "optimizing function."
- It requires the alignment of two distinct elements:
  1. The individual's Intrinsic Capacity.
  2. The Environment they inhabit.
- When capacity and environment align, the result is high Functional Ability.

SLIDE 9: INTRINSIC CAPACITY AND FUNCTIONAL ABILITY
The Core Equation of Geriatric Health
- Intrinsic Capacity: The composite of all the physical and mental capacities of an individual (e.g., locomotion, vitality, cognition, psychological well-being).
- Environment: The external factors (built environment, social support, policies, assistive technologies).
- Functional Ability: The interaction between capacity and environment. 
- Example: A person with low locomotion capacity can still have high functional ability to shop for groceries if they have a motorized wheelchair and live in a neighborhood with accessible stores and ramps.

SLIDE 10: THE PHYSICAL ENVIRONMENT
Age-Friendly Cities
- Urban design must accommodate the sensory, mobility, and cognitive changes associated with aging.
- Features: Longer pedestrian crossing times, abundant public seating, clear and large signage, well-lit streets, and accessible public transit.
- The 8 Domains of the WHO Age-Friendly City: Outdoor spaces, transportation, housing, social participation, respect and social inclusion, civic participation, communication, and community support.
- Takeaway: A city that is designed to be safe and accessible for an 80-year-old is a city that is safe and accessible for everyone.

SLIDE 11: AGING IN PLACE
The Desire to Remain Home
- The strong preference of most older adults to remain in their own homes and communities as they age, rather than moving to institutional care.
- Enablers of Aging in Place: Home modifications (grab bars, stairlifts), community-based care (visiting nurses, meal delivery), and strong neighborhood social capital.
- Barriers: Large, multi-story homes that become physically unmanageable, lack of public transit leading to driving cessation and subsequent isolation.
- HP Application: Health promotion must advocate for "visitability" and universal design in all new housing construction.

SLIDE 12: THE EPIDEMIC OF LONELINESS
Isolation vs. Loneliness
- Social isolation (objective lack of social contacts) and loneliness (subjective feeling of being alone) are distinct but related crises.
- Up to 25 percent of older adults experience severe social isolation.
- Drivers: Retirement, loss of a spouse, mobility limitations, hearing loss, and the geographic dispersion of families.
- The modern built environment, which prioritizes cars over pedestrians, severely limits the spontaneous social interactions that sustain older adults.

SLIDE 13: LONELINESS AS A BIOLOGICAL TOXIN
The Mortality Risk of Disconnection
- Chronic loneliness triggers a persistent "fight or flight" stress response.
- It increases systemic inflammation (elevated C-reactive protein), impairs immune function, and accelerates cognitive decline.
- Meta-analyses show that the mortality risk of chronic social isolation is equivalent to smoking 15 cigarettes a day, and exceeds the risks of obesity and physical inactivity.
- Takeaway: Social connection is a fundamental biological need, not just a psychological preference.

SLIDE 14: INTERGENERATIONAL SOLIDARITY
Bridging the Age Gap
- Segregating society by age (schools for the young, retirement communities for the old) destroys social cohesion and fuels ageism.
- Intergenerational programs bring different age groups together in purposeful, mutually beneficial activities.
- Examples: Co-locating preschools inside nursing homes, university students living rent-free in elder care facilities in exchange for companionship, and mentorship programs.
- Outcomes: Reduces ageism in youth, reduces depression and cognitive decline in elders, and builds community resilience.

SLIDE 15: POLYPHARMACY
The Medication Regimen as a Hazard
- Polypharmacy is the concurrent use of multiple medications (typically 5 or more) by a single individual.
- Driven by the siloed nature of healthcare: a cardiologist prescribes for the heart, an endocrinologist for diabetes, a psychiatrist for sleep, without a central coordinator.
- Risks: Severe adverse drug reactions, drug-drug interactions, cognitive clouding, and high rates of falls due to dizziness or orthostatic hypotension.
- Takeaway: In the elderly, the medication regimen itself often becomes a primary determinant of poor health.

SLIDE 16: DEPRESCRIBING AS HEALTH PROMOTION
Doing Less to Achieve More
- Deprescribing is the systematic, supervised process of tapering or stopping medications that are no longer beneficial or may be causing harm.
- It requires shifting the clinical mindset from "adding a pill for every symptom" to "optimizing the overall regimen."
- Health promoters must empower older adults to ask their doctors: "Do I still need this? What are the risks of stopping?"
- This is a prime example of tertiary prevention and reorienting health services.

SLIDE 17: DEMENTIA AND COGNITIVE DECLINE
A Public Health Approach
- Dementia is not a normal part of aging; it is a syndrome caused by various brain illnesses (e.g., Alzheimer's, vascular dementia).
- Historically viewed as an inevitable, untreatable biological decline.
- The modern public health paradigm views dementia through a life-course and preventive lens.
- While we cannot yet cure the neuropathology, we can significantly delay the onset and reduce the overall population burden through upstream health promotion.

SLIDE 18: MODIFIABLE RISK FACTORS FOR DEMENTIA
The Lancet Commission Findings
- The Lancet Commission identified 12 modifiable risk factors that account for roughly 40 percent of worldwide dementias.
- Early life: Less education.
- Midlife: Hearing loss, traumatic brain injury, hypertension, alcohol misuse, obesity.
- Later life: Smoking, depression, social isolation, physical inactivity, air pollution, diabetes.
- HP Application: Dementia prevention does not start at age 70; it starts in childhood education and midlife cardiovascular health.

SLIDE 19: THE MYTH OF THE "BURDEN"
Reframing the Economic Narrative
- The narrative that an aging population will bankrupt healthcare systems and crush the working-age generation.
- This narrative relies on the "dependency ratio," which assumes everyone over 65 is economically and physically dependent.
- Reality: Older adults are the largest providers of unpaid labor in society (childcare for grandchildren, volunteering, and caring for their own aging spouses).
- Framing the elderly as a "burden" justifies ageist policies and ignores their massive economic and social contributions.

SLIDE 20: THE HIDDEN PATIENTS: INFORMAL CAREGIVERS
The Cost of Caring
- The vast majority of care for older adults is provided by informal, unpaid caregivers (spouses, adult children).
- Caregiver Burnout: The chronic stress of caregiving leads to severe depression, compromised immune function, and increased mortality for the caregiver.
- Health promotion must target the caregiver as a secondary patient.
- Interventions: Respite care, caregiver support groups, workplace policies allowing paid family leave, and financial subsidies.

SLIDE 21: ELDER ABUSE AND NEGLECT
A Hidden Public Health Crisis
- A deeply underreported crisis affecting roughly 1 in 6 older adults globally.
- Forms: Physical, psychological, sexual, financial exploitation, and neglect (both by others and self-neglect).
- Risk Factors: Social isolation of the elder, caregiver stress, cognitive impairment, and financial dependency of the abuser on the elder.
- HP Application: Mandatory reporting training for healthcare workers, community awareness campaigns, and strengthening adult protective services.

SLIDE 22: INTERSECTIONALITY IN LATER LIFE
Cumulative Disadvantage
- Aging does not erase earlier life disadvantages; it magnifies them.
- An older adult who faced lifelong racial discrimination, gender pay gaps, or disability will enter old age with significantly less wealth, poorer housing, and worse baseline health.
- LGBTQ+ elders often face the "crisis of care," fearing discrimination in long-term care facilities and lacking traditional family support networks.
- Health promotion for the elderly must be deeply intersectional to avoid only serving the privileged.

SLIDE 23: FALL PREVENTION THROUGH THE SEM
A Multi-Level Approach
- Falls are the leading cause of fatal and non-fatal injuries in older adults.
- Intrapersonal: Muscle weakness, poor vision, polypharmacy.
- Interpersonal: Lack of a caregiver to assist with risky transfers.
- Institutional: Hospitals discharging patients to unsafe home environments.
- Community: Lack of safe, paved sidewalks; poor street lighting.
- Policy: Lack of insurance coverage for home modifications and physical therapy.

SLIDE 24: THE DIGITAL DIVIDE IN AGING
Systemic Exclusion
- As healthcare, banking, and social services move online, older adults without digital literacy or broadband access are systematically excluded.
- This digital exclusion exacerbates social isolation and limits access to telehealth and social benefits.
- Health promotion must include digital literacy training, subsidized internet access, and the preservation of non-digital (analog) alternatives for essential services.

SLIDE 25: THE TRAP OF "AGE-TECH"
Technology vs. Human Connection
- The rapid proliferation of technologies designed for the elderly (e.g., fall-detection pendants, robotic companions, remote monitoring sensors).
- Critique: Tech is often used by governments and families as a cheap substitute for human care and social connection.
- A robotic seal might reduce agitation in dementia, but it does not replace the human need for touch and genuine relationship.
- Technology should enable human connection, not replace it.

SLIDE 26: APPLYING PRECEDE-PROCEED TO THE ELDERLY
Diagnosing the Context
- Phase 1 (Social): The desire to maintain independence, dignity, and connection to the community.
- Phase 2 (Epidemiological): High rates of falls, depression, and polypharmacy complications.
- Phase 3 (Educational/Ecological):
  - Predisposing: Fear of falling leading to self-restricted mobility; belief that depression is "normal" in old age.
  - Enabling: Lack of accessible transport; unaffordable home modifications.
  - Reinforcing: Ageist media portrayals; overworked caregivers lacking time to encourage physical activity.

SLIDE 27: APPLYING THE OTTAWA CHARTER TO AGING
Structural Interventions
- Build Healthy Public Policy: Pension adequacy, mandatory paid caregiver leave, and universal design building codes.
- Create Supportive Environments: Age-friendly cities, intergenerational housing, and accessible green spaces.
- Strengthen Community Action: Neighborhood volunteer networks for snow shoveling, grocery delivery, and social check-ins.
- Develop Personal Skills: Digital literacy classes, chronic disease self-management programs.
- Reorient Health Services: Shifting from acute, hospital-based care to comprehensive, home-based geriatric care teams.

SLIDE 28: FOOD FOR THOUGHT
The Anti-Aging Industry
- The multi-billion-dollar "anti-aging" industry sells creams, supplements, and extreme diets promising to reverse the clock.
- This industry is built entirely on the pathologizing and stigmatizing of natural aging.
- It promotes a toxic, unattainable ideal of "agelessness" that fuels internalized ageism and diverts resources away from actual health-span extension and social support.
- Reflection: True health promotion embraces aging as a natural, valuable stage of life, rather than a cosmetic defect to be erased.

SLIDE 29: PUZZLE
The Ethics of Resource Allocation
- Scenario: A new, highly effective but extremely expensive drug is developed that extends the life of 85-year-olds with advanced heart failure by an average of 6 months, but does not improve their daily quality of life or functional capacity.
- Question: From a public health and utilitarian perspective, should a universal healthcare system fund this drug, or redirect those funds to early childhood nutrition programs that yield a 50-year health span return?
- How do we balance the moral imperative to care for the vulnerable elderly with the population-level need for upstream prevention?

SLIDE 30: SUMMARY OF CORE CONCEPTS
The Takeaways
- Aging is a public health triumph, not a demographic crisis.
- Ageism is a lethal social determinant that accelerates biological decline.
- Healthy aging is the interaction between Intrinsic Capacity and the Environment.
- Social isolation is a biological toxin equivalent to smoking.
- Polypharmacy is a major iatrogenic risk requiring active deprescribing.
- Dementia prevention requires a life-course approach, starting in midlife.

SLIDE 31: KEY TERMS GLOSSARY
Master the Vocabulary
- Ageism: Stereotyping, prejudice, and discrimination based on age.
- Intrinsic Capacity: The composite of physical and mental capacities of an individual.
- Functional Ability: The interaction between intrinsic capacity and the environment.
- Aging in Place: The ability to live in one's own home and community safely and independently.
- Polypharmacy: The concurrent use of multiple medications, increasing the risk of adverse events.
- Deprescribing: The supervised process of tapering or stopping inappropriate medications.
- Intergenerational Solidarity: Purposeful, mutually beneficial interactions between different age cohorts.
