SLIDE 3: THE ARCHITECTURE OF THE FINAL REPORT
The Simulated Grant Proposal
- You are stepping into the role of a Lead Health Promoter applying for funding.
- The report is divided into two distinct parts to mirror real-world project management.
- Part 1 (Due Week 5): The Diagnosis and Theoretical Framework. (Why is this a problem, and what drives it?)
- Part 2 (Due Week 12): The Intervention, Implementation, and Evaluation Plan. (What are we going to do about it, and how will we measure success?)
Takeaway: You cannot design a solution (Part 2) until you have rigorously proven and diagnosed the problem (Part 1).

SLIDE 4: WHY SPLIT THE REPORT INTO TWO PARTS?
The Danger of Premature Solutions
- In the real world, public health professionals often rush to implement a "pet solution" (e.g., "Let's build an app!") before understanding the community.
- By forcing you to submit Part 1 first, I am forcing you to sit with the data, listen to the community, and map the theoretical drivers of the issue.
- You will receive feedback on Part 1. You must use this feedback to refine your diagnosis before designing your intervention in Part 2.
- If your Part 1 diagnosis is flawed, your Part 2 intervention will fail.

SLIDE 5: PART 1 FOCUS - THE DIAGNOSIS
Phases 1, 2, and 3 of PRECEDE-PROCEED
- Part 1 is strictly limited to the diagnostic phases.
- Phase 1: Social Assessment (Quality of Life).
- Phase 2: Epidemiological Assessment (Health Goals).
- Phase 3: Educational and Ecological Assessment (Predisposing, Enabling, Reinforcing factors).
- You are not allowed to propose your final intervention in Part 1. You are only allowed to identify the targets for change.
Takeaway: Part 1 is the "detective work." Part 2 is the "treatment plan."

SLIDE 6: SELECTING YOUR TARGET POPULATION
Who Are You Trying to Help?
- A target population must be specific, defined, and accessible.
- Bad Example: "Adults in the country." (Too broad, impossible to tailor).
- Bad Example: "People who are sick." (Not a demographic).
- Good Example: "First-generation immigrant women aged 30 to 50 living in the [Specific] urban district."
- Good Example: "Shift-workers at the [Specific] regional manufacturing plant."
Rule: You must be able to describe their daily lived reality, their socioeconomic status, and their cultural context.

SLIDE 7: SELECTING YOUR HEALTH ISSUE
What is the Specific Burden?
- The health issue must be directly linked to the target population's quality of life.
- It must be a public health issue, not purely a clinical one.
- Bad Example: "Curing rare genetic mutations." (Clinical/genetic, not suitable for health promotion).
- Good Example: "High rates of unmanaged Type 2 Diabetes leading to preventable ER visits."
- Good Example: "Elevated rates of burnout and depressive symptoms among nursing staff."
Rule: The issue must be influenced by behavioral, environmental, or social determinants.

SLIDE 8: THE GOLDILOCKS RULE OF SCOPE
Not Too Broad, Not Too Narrow
- Too Broad: "Ending childhood obesity in the nation." (You cannot solve this in a single report; the drivers are too vast).
- Too Narrow: "Convincing one specific teenager to eat an apple." (Not a public health issue).
- Just Right: "Reducing the consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages among middle school students in [Specific] low-income district."
Takeaway: Your scope must be narrow enough to allow for a deep, rigorous PRECEDE-PROCEED analysis, but broad enough to represent a genuine community health burden.

SLIDE 9: PHASE 1 - SOCIAL ASSESSMENT IN PRACTICE
Defining the Community's Priorities
- Your report must begin by establishing the social context of your target population.
- What are their primary social concerns? (e.g., housing insecurity, unemployment, crime, lack of transit, social isolation).
- How does the health issue you selected impact these social concerns?
- Example: If your population is elderly immigrants, their primary social concern might be "isolation and loss of independence." Your health issue (e.g., fall risk) must be framed as a barrier to their social goal of independent living.

SLIDE 10: WHERE TO FIND SOCIAL DATA
Moving Beyond Anecdotes
- You cannot rely on assumptions or stereotypes about your target population.
- Use census data, municipal reports, local NGO needs assessments, and qualitative community surveys.
- Look for data on: Income distribution, educational attainment, housing quality, employment rates, and community infrastructure.
- Pro Tip: Quote local community leaders or published qualitative studies to capture the "voice" and lived experience of the population.

SLIDE 11: PHASE 2 - EPIDEMIOLOGICAL ASSESSMENT IN PRACTICE
Quantifying the Health Burden
- Now, narrow the social goals down to specific health outcomes.
- You must provide hard epidemiological data proving the problem exists and is severe.
- Metrics to include: Prevalence, incidence, mortality rates, morbidity rates, and Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs).
- Disaggregate the data: Do not just give national averages. Show how the rates in your specific target population compare to the general population to highlight health inequities.

SLIDE 12: WHERE TO FIND EPIDEMIOLOGICAL DATA
The Gold Standard Sources
- National and local health departments (e.g., CDC, WHO, Ministry of Health).
- Peer-reviewed epidemiological journals.
- Disease-specific registries (e.g., Cancer Registries, Diabetes Surveillance Systems).
- Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study data.
Takeaway: Every claim about the severity of the health issue must be backed by a credible, recent, and citable statistic.

SLIDE 13: WRITING THE PROBLEM STATEMENT
The Core of Part 1
- By the end of Phase 2, you must write a formal Problem Statement.
- Formula: [Target Population] experiences a disproportionate burden of [Health Issue], as evidenced by [Statistic], which negatively impacts their [Social Goal/Quality of Life].
- Example: "Shift-workers at the regional plant experience a 30 percent higher rate of cardiovascular disease compared to day-workers, severely impacting their economic stability and family well-being."
- This statement acts as the anchor for the rest of your report.

SLIDE 14: PHASE 3 - BEHAVIORAL AND ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS
The Bridge to Intervention
- Phase 3 is the most critical section for your grade.
- You must identify the specific behaviors and environmental conditions that cause the health issue identified in Phase 2.
- Behavioral Factors: What are the people doing (or not doing) that increases their risk? (e.g., smoking, sedentary lifestyle, skipping medication).
- Environmental Factors: What is happening in their physical, social, or economic environment that drives the health issue? (e.g., food deserts, toxic workplace culture, lack of green space).

SLIDE 15: DISTINGUISHING BEHAVIOR FROM ENVIRONMENT
A Common Student Error
- Students often confuse the two or blame the individual for environmental failures.
- Example: "The behavior is eating fast food." 
- Correction: Eating fast food is the behavior. But WHY are they doing it? If they live in a food desert and work two jobs, the ENVIRONMENTAL factors are lack of grocery stores and lack of time.
- Your intervention will eventually target the environment, not just the behavior. You must map both accurately.

SLIDE 16: PREDISPOSING FACTORS - THE LITERATURE SEARCH
Mapping the Mind and Beliefs
- What knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and values drive the target behaviors?
- Use the Health Belief Model (HBM) constructs here.
- Do they lack knowledge about the disease? (Knowledge)
- Do they believe the disease is inevitable? (Perceived Susceptibility/Severity)
- Do they lack confidence in their ability to change? (Self-Efficacy)
- You must cite peer-reviewed literature proving that these specific predisposing factors are present in your target population.

SLIDE 17: ENABLING FACTORS - THE LITERATURE SEARCH
Mapping the Resources and Barriers
- What skills, resources, or systemic barriers allow or prevent the behavior?
- Use the Social Ecological Model (SEM) here (Institutional, Community, Policy levels).
- Do they lack the financial resources to buy healthy food? (Economic barrier)
- Is the clinic only open during their working hours? (Structural barrier)
- Do they lack the physical skills to cook from scratch? (Skill deficit)
Takeaway: Enabling factors are usually the most powerful leverage points for health promotion.

SLIDE 18: REINFORCING FACTORS - THE LITERATURE SEARCH
Mapping the Social Feedback
- What rewards or punishments follow the behavior?
- Who influences the target population? (Family, peers, bosses, media).
- If a teenager tries to quit vaping, do their friends support them or mock them? (Peer reinforcement).
- If a worker takes a mental health day, does the manager praise them or penalize them? (Organizational reinforcement).
- Cite sociological or psychological studies that map the social networks and norms of your population.

SLIDE 19: BUILDING THE CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK
Visualizing Your Diagnosis
- Part 1 must include a visual diagram (a Conceptual Framework or Logic Model of the problem).
- This diagram should visually map the flow from Social Goals -> Health Issue -> Behaviors/Environments -> Predisposing/Enabling/Reinforcing Factors.
- This proves to the grader that you understand how all the theoretical pieces connect logically.
- A messy or illogical diagram indicates a flawed diagnosis.

SLIDE 20: THE ROLE OF THE LITERATURE REVIEW
Not Just a Summary
- The literature review in Part 1 is not a book report. It is an evidence-gathering mission.
- You are reviewing the literature to answer one question: "What does the scientific evidence say drives this health issue in this specific type of population?"
- Every factor you list in Phase 3 (Predisposing, Enabling, Reinforcing) must be justified by at least two high-quality, peer-reviewed citations.

SLIDE 21: SYNTHESIZING VS. SUMMARIZING
How to Write Like a Scholar
- Summarizing (Bad): "Smith (2019) found that poverty causes stress. Jones (2020) found that stress causes heart disease."
- Synthesizing (Good): "The pathway from economic disadvantage to cardiovascular risk is heavily mediated by chronic allostatic load, as demonstrated by both Smith (2019) and Jones (2020), suggesting that enabling financial resources is a critical upstream intervention."
Takeaway: Group your citations by theoretical themes (e.g., "Barriers to Access," "Cultural Beliefs"), not by author.

SLIDE 22: FOOD FOR THOUGHT - POSITIONALITY AND BIAS
Who is Doing the Diagnosing?
- As a health promoter, you hold power. You are defining what is "normal" and what is "pathological" in a community.
- Question: How does your own background (class, race, education, health status) bias your perception of the community's needs?
- If you are an outsider to the community, how are you ensuring that your "diagnosis" reflects their actual lived reality and not your own stereotypes?
- Reflection: True needs assessments require humility and a willingness to be corrected by the community.

SLIDE 23: EPISTEMIC INJUSTICE IN NEEDS ASSESSMENTS
Whose Data Counts?
- In Lecture 2, we discussed Epistemic Injustice (wronging someone in their capacity as a knower).
- When conducting your Phase 1 Social Assessment, are you only looking at quantitative government data?
- Government data often misses marginalized groups (e.g., undocumented immigrants, homeless populations).
- You must actively seek out qualitative data, community narratives, and grassroots reports to avoid committing hermeneutical injustice against your target population.

SLIDE 24: COMMON PITFALL 1 - THE PET SOLUTION
Jumping to Conclusions
- The most common reason students lose points in Part 1 is proposing the solution before finishing the diagnosis.
- Example: A student wants to build a community garden. They choose "obesity" as their topic, and then manipulate the Phase 3 data to make it look like a lack of gardening is the primary cause of obesity.
- This is intellectually dishonest and leads to bad public health.
- Rule: Let the data dictate the solution. Do not force the data to fit your favorite idea.

SLIDE 25: COMMON PITFALL 2 - THE DEFICIT NARRATIVE
Ignoring Community Assets
- A "deficit narrative" focuses only on what a community lacks (disease, poverty, ignorance, bad habits).
- This is demoralizing and ignores the community's resilience and existing strengths.
- Asset-Based Community Development (ABCD): Your Phase 1 and 3 assessments must also identify the community's existing assets (e.g., strong church networks, resilient cultural traditions, active local leaders).
- These assets will become the "Enabling" and "Reinforcing" factors you leverage in Part 2.

SLIDE 26: COMMON PITFALL 3 - THE DATA DUMP
Losing the Narrative Thread
- Do not just paste pages of statistics and tables into your report.
- Data without context is meaningless. 
- Every statistic you include must be explicitly tied back to the narrative of your target population and the specific health issue.
- Use the "So What?" test. After every paragraph of data, ask yourself: "So what? Why does this matter to the problem statement?" If you cannot answer, delete the data.

SLIDE 27: ACADEMIC WRITING STANDARDS FOR PART 1
Professionalism and Clarity
- Tone: Objective, professional, empathetic, and analytical. Avoid emotional pleading or colloquialisms.
- Structure: Use clear headings aligned with the PRECEDE-PROCEED phases.
- Formatting: Follow the university guidelines strictly (font, margins, line spacing).
- Proofreading: Grammatical errors and typos signal a lack of attention to detail, which is a fatal flaw in public health planning.

SLIDE 28: CITATION AND EVIDENCE GRADING
Proving Your Claims
- Use the required citation style (e.g., APA, Harvard) flawlessly.
- Evidence Hierarchy: Prioritize systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and peer-reviewed primary studies.
- Grey Literature: Government reports, WHO documents, and reputable NGO reports are acceptable for Phase 1 and 2 data, but Phase 3 theoretical drivers must be backed by peer-reviewed science.
- Do not use Wikipedia, commercial blogs, or unverified news articles as sources.

SLIDE 29: PUZZLING QUESTIONS FOR REFLECTION
The Ethics of the Needs Assessment
1. If the community's stated social priority (Phase 1) conflicts with the epidemiological data (Phase 2) - e.g., the community wants a new sports stadium, but the data shows a massive spike in infectious disease - whose priority should the health promoter follow?
2. How do we conduct a "needs assessment" without reducing a vibrant, complex human community to a list of medicalized deficits and risk factors?
3. If the literature review reveals that the only effective intervention is a massive wealth redistribution policy, but your funding agency only supports individual-level education, how do you write Part 1 ethically?

SLIDE 30: THE PART 1 CHECKLIST
Before You Submit, Verify:
- Is my target population specific and clearly defined?

- Is my Problem Statement clear, concise, and compelling?
